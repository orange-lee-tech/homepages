## 01 | An exchange is more than studying in a different place

Of course, the most visible part of the exchange was taking courses. What stayed with me more deeply, however, was that learning, living, and time management all had to be rebuilt from the first day in a new environment.

Course registration, email-based notices, academic systems, budgeting, commuting, and exam schedules all had to be handled more proactively. In that sense, the exchange functioned like a compact training ground for independent living. I was not only completing courses; I was also learning to structure time, process change, and take responsibility for my own rhythm.

## 02 | Coursework expanded my sense of what engineering ability includes

Among the three courses, Engineering Economics had the strongest impact on me, while Calculus (II) and Probability and Statistics further consolidated my mathematical foundations. Together, they raised a broader question: how should an engineering student use knowledge to judge real-world problems?

Engineering Economics, in particular, shifted my perspective. I used to understand engineering problems mainly through models, principles, and implementation. This course placed cash flow, payback period, uncertainty, and project choice alongside technical feasibility. It reminded me that an engineering solution is not only about whether something can be built, but also whether it should be built and how resources should be judged.

## 03 | Beyond the course itself, I turned that learning into a study-group practice

There was another thread in this exchange that I wanted to preserve more clearly. While at Yuan Ze University, I also took part in an economics-themed study group that grew out of my Engineering Economics learning. It was not just a side activity. It extended classroom thinking into reading, discussion, and repeated reflection.

The series ran for six sessions. We discussed books such as <em>Rich Dad Poor Dad</em>, <em>From 0 to Financial Freedom</em>, <em>A Dog Called Money</em>, and <em>A Dog Called Money 2</em>, while talking about credit cards, housing, investment discipline, wealth standards, entrepreneurship, student loans, and asset allocation. To me, its value was not simply that we “read some books,” but that we put abstract ideas about money, engineering judgment, and long-term decisions back into everyday life.

In the end, the study group won second prize with a pre-tax cash award of NT$ 8,000. There is no need to exaggerate that result. It simply shows that classroom knowledge, when continued through discussion and sustained effort, can produce visible outcomes.

## 04 | The exchange left more than credits behind

Beyond courses, the 128 days also trained me to observe campus systems, student services, and everyday routines with more structure. From campus events and peer interaction to transport, convenience stores, receipts, and communication habits, many small differences ultimately returned me to one central question: how to remain open, steady, and self-managed in a different environment.

I gradually learned to consider living costs, course review, future planning, and personal judgment as parts of the same system. That is why this exchange matters to me not just as “one semester completed,” but as a visible turning point in how I want to keep learning and building long-term capability assets.
